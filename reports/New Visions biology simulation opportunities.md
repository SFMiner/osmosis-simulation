# Build simulations around hidden biological mechanisms

**The strongest next builds are a body-water-to-cell bridge, a light/dark plant gas-exchange lab, and a mountain-lion genetic-rescue model.** Each has a specific home in New Visions Biology and extends an investigation students already do. Your osmosis simulator follows the same productive pattern: use a physical lab to establish evidence, then make the invisible mechanism available for students to manipulate. One clarification matters: the course containing Marathon Runner is officially **high-school Biology**; this research did not find a separate New Visions middle-school biology course. The recommendations below adapt that course for your middle-school audience. Current unit overviews establish the six-unit sequence, while accessible lesson details come from several explicitly dated editions. These are proposed supplements, not claims that New Visions endorses these designs or lacks equivalent tools behind its registration system. ([Official course sequence](https://www.newvisions.org/curriculum/biology-curricular-materials))

## The six units offer different simulation opportunities

The course uses phenomena to organize explanations, giving a small simulation a clear purpose beyond demonstrating a biology topic. The current sequence is below; the final column is my design judgment, while the unit names and storylines come from New Visions.

| Unit | Verified storyline and instructional context | Promising simulation question |
|---|---|---|
| [1. Marathon Runner](https://www.newvisions.org/curriculum/science/biology/marathon-runner) | A runner becomes disoriented and comatose. Gas exchange, muscles and energy, thermoregulation, and water balance build an explanation involving interacting systems. | How do drinking, water removal, and sweating change blood concentration and cell volume? |
| [2. Humans vs. Bacteria](https://www.newvisions.org/curriculum/science/biology/humans-vs-bacteria) | Black Death, antibiotic resistance, the microbiome, and cooperation lead toward preventing a future cholera outbreak. | How does an antibiotic change the proportions of already-varying bacteria across generations? |
| [3. Evolution of Sick Humans](https://www.newvisions.org/curriculum/science/biology/evolution-of-sick-humans) | Lactase persistence, leptin resistance, circadian rhythms, and common ancestry inform a healthy-school design. | How does enzyme availability change lactose breakdown, without implying that need creates inherited traits? |
| [4. Saving the Mountain Lion](https://www.newvisions.org/curriculum/science/biology/saving-the-mountain-lion) | A Connecticut mountain lion's origins introduce populations, optional sexual reproduction, genetic variation, and engineering gene flow. | How do immigration and habitat connectivity change variation over generations? |
| [5. Food for All](https://www.newvisions.org/curriculum/science/biology/food-for-all) | The Neolithic Revolution, corn, pellagra, food processing, nutrition, and food for plants connect matter and energy to inequitable access to food. | What changes inside a plant container when light switches off? |
| [6. Woolly Mammoth](https://www.newvisions.org/curriculum/science/biology/woolly-mammoth) | Tuskless elephants, coral bleaching, kelp forests, and passenger pigeons support reasoning about extinction and de-extinction. | Why does a disturbed ecosystem sometimes fail to recover when the disturbance ends? |

**Middle-school accessibility is a design constraint here, not a verified publisher alignment.** Preserve the phenomenon, but begin with visible objects, one manipulated variable, and an observable comparison. Hide optional vocabulary and numerical detail until needed. Antibiotic selection and lactase are reasonable candidates, but this research verified only their current storyline placement, not the detailed activities or existing simulations. They therefore rank below the better-documented opportunities that follow.

New Visions distinguishes its current Biology course from legacy Living Environment materials. Mixing those sequences would produce misleading lesson placements. Registration is free, but several detailed material links led to registration or sign-in; the linked SY26–27 Marathon Runner unit plan did not expose readable content. The findings below should therefore be checked against your actual teaching edition before building a lesson-specific worksheet. ## The best three builds extend investigations directly

**First: connect whole-body water balance to your existing cell model.** This is the clearest immediate extension. In the SY25–26 Marathon Runner student materials, the onion investigation occupies printed pages 132–134: students observe cells, add salt solution and then distilled water, and model concentration and net water movement. Pages 135–138 move directly into kidney input/output evidence for different water intakes during exercise. Students compare blood and urine, explain kidney behavior, and predict sports-drink effects. The adjacency makes a body-to-cell bridge unusually well matched to the actual sequence. ([SY25–26 student materials](https://newvisions-uploads-production.s3.amazonaws.com/bio_U1/BIO%20%7C%20U1%20%7C%20Marathon%20Runner%20%7C%20Student%20Materials%20%7C%20SY%2025-26.pdf))

The proposed model asks, **“How can drinking water eventually change a cell's size?”** Students add water, change activity, and compare low, moderate, and high intake scenarios. A blood reservoir shows water amount and explicitly tracked solute; a linked animal cell changes size as the surrounding concentration changes. Water removal appears as urine, with a simple regulated-versus-fixed-removal comparison reserved for a second investigation. Display concentration and cell volume over time. The core misconception is that *amount of solute* and *concentration* mean the same thing; another is that every additional drink necessarily brings a system closer to normal.

The minimum useful version is one reservoir, one cell, three scenarios, and a saved graph comparison. Reuse the current cell visualization where suitable. Keep onion-cell and animal-cell structural behavior distinct, and label the body model as qualitative: urine formation cannot be represented as passive osmosis alone. If salt loss is omitted, say so; if included, account for it explicitly. Students should distinguish curriculum measurements from the model's generated results. Detailed kidney anatomy and calibrated hormone dynamics would enlarge the project without being necessary for the first causal comparison.

**Second: make a paired light/dark plant experiment.** This is the closest analogue to your successful onion-lab supplement. The accessible SY22–23 Food for All teacher guide includes light/dark plant investigations using carbon-dioxide and oxygen observations. Its lesson placement is in *The SuperFood That Changed the World*; confirm this against your newer edition rather than assigning it from the broader “Food for Plants” title alone. ([SY22–23 teacher materials, p.48](https://newvisions-uploads-production.s3.amazonaws.com/bio_U5/BIO%20%7C%20U5%20%7C%20Food%20for%20All%20%7C%20Teacher%20Materials%20%7C%20SY%2022-23.pdf))

Two otherwise identical closed containers let students switch one into darkness and compare gas changes. Show **photosynthesis and respiration as separate, simultaneous processes**, alongside a simple sugar store and CO₂/O₂ graphs. Begin with light as the only control; plant quantity and starting carbon dioxide can follow. The important result is a net change produced by competing processes, not a memorized “plants take in CO₂” arrow. Target the misconceptions that plants respire only at night and that most plant carbon comes from soil. The minimum version needs two containers, light toggles, pause/reset, and a stored comparison. Omit biochemical pathways and avoid forcing a realistic numerical rate unless the model has been independently checked.

**Third: turn mountain-lion genetic rescue into repeated generations.** In the SY24–25 Saving the Mountain Lion materials, Engineering Gene Flow already uses colored marbles, bags, and a genetic-diversity calculator to investigate introducing Texas individuals into Florida. A digital version earns its place by adding inheritance across generations, connectivity, and repeatable trials—not by recreating the marble procedure on screen. ([SY24–25 teacher materials, pp.99–100](https://newvisions-uploads-production.s3.amazonaws.com/bio_U4/BIO%20%7C%20U4%20%7C%20Saving%20the%20Mountain%20Lion%20%7C%20Teacher%20Materials%20%7C%20SY%2024-25.pdf))

Start with two habitat patches and one inherited marker represented by colored allele tokens. Students choose whether populations connect, how many immigrants arrive, and which variants those immigrants carry. Offspring receive one allele from each parent; five generations and several repeated runs reveal changes and chance differences. Show allele variety and paired alleles, with optional heterozygosity detail. The key comparison is **adding individuals versus adding different inherited variants**. A corridor redistributes existing alleles; it does not manufacture mutations. The minimum version should report variation at the modeled marker rather than pretend one marker predicts actual mountain-lion survival or overall population health. This offers a different scientific domain from osmosis while retaining concrete, manipulable objects.

## Three further builds address feedback, food webs, and matter

**Fourth: build glucose feedback around meals and activity.** Marathon Runner's Muscles & Energy sequence already includes a chemical-cup simulation of glucose regulation in the accessible SY25–26 teacher materials. Your added value would be translating that symbolic procedure into glucose moving among digestion, blood, cells, and storage. Students add a meal, increase activity, and compare functioning feedback with a simplified disabled response. One graph should fluctuate around a normal band, making clear that homeostasis does not mean a perfectly flat line. Glucose tokens should move or transform explicitly rather than disappear when “insulin” turns on. The minimum version is a meal button, activity setting, feedback toggle, and one graph; avoid medication dosing or treatment mechanics. ([SY25–26 teacher materials, p.98](https://newvisions-uploads-production.s3.amazonaws.com/bio_U1/BIO%20%7C%20U1%20%7C%20Marathon%20Runner%20%7C%20Teacher%20Materials%20%7C%20SY%2025-26.pdf))

**Fifth: test kelp-forest disturbance and recovery.** The accessible SY22–23 Woolly Mammoth guide's Kelp & Barrens investigation uses maps, disturbance histories, ecosystem cards, and stable states. A small model can extend that evidence across time. Students compare a baseline with predator loss or an urchin-removal intervention, tracking kelp cover, grazers, and predators. Start with one disturbance and one restoration action; preserve side-by-side runs. The useful misconception is that ending a disturbance guarantees an immediate return to the original ecosystem. Recovery thresholds belong in a later version only if their assumptions are explicit. Do not present the model as proof of the cause at a historical site, or assume every kelp ecosystem has the same predator relationships. ([SY22–23 teacher materials, pp.94–100](https://newvisions-uploads-production.s3.amazonaws.com/bio_U6/BIO%20%7C%20U6%20%7C%20Woolly%20Mammoth%20%7C%20Teacher%20Materials%20%7C%20SY%2022-23.pdf))

**Sixth: track where decomposing food goes.** Food for All uses food-decomposition time-lapse evidence in the accessible SY22–23 teacher guide. A complementary model would follow food carbon into decomposers and carbon dioxide while showing water separately. Students change moisture or temperature and choose a system boundary: food alone versus the whole sealed container. Observations include visible food remaining, decomposer material, gas, and total mass within that boundary. This directly challenges “the matter disappeared.” The minimum version needs one generic food, a carbon ledger, and a boundary toggle. Do not infer nutritional quality from decay rate or encode processed food as inherently unable to decompose; those would be unsupported lessons. ([SY22–23 teacher materials, p.75](https://newvisions-uploads-production.s3.amazonaws.com/bio_U5/BIO%20%7C%20U5%20%7C%20Food%20for%20All%20%7C%20Teacher%20Materials%20%7C%20SY%2022-23.pdf))

## Existing resources narrow what is worth building

Generic population growth is a lower priority: Food for All's SY25–26 student materials already assign Jon Darkow's *Population Dynamics of White Footed Mouse*, asking students to change environmental variables and record effects. Woolly Mammoth's older teacher materials also assign EcoOcean and a carbon-cycle simulation. These references establish existing assignments, not a completed usability assessment of those tools. ([SY25–26 Food for All student materials, p.20](https://newvisions-uploads-production.s3.amazonaws.com/bio_U5/BIO%20%7C%20U5%20%7C%20Food%20for%20All%20%7C%20Student%20Materials%20%7C%20SY%2025-26.pdf); [assigned mouse model](https://exchange.iseesystems.com/public/jondarkow/population-dynamics-of-white-footed-mouse/index.html#page2); [SY22–23 Woolly Mammoth teacher materials](https://newvisions-uploads-production.s3.amazonaws.com/bio_U6/BIO%20%7C%20U6%20%7C%20Woolly%20Mammoth%20%7C%20Teacher%20Materials%20%7C%20SY%2022-23.pdf))

Coral bleaching likewise deserves an existing-resource check first. HHMI offers an animation and an interactive treatment with questions and supporting content, and NASA lists a game in which learners change temperature and pollution. A new coral model would need a specific advantage, such as comparing heat-pulse duration and recovery over time. ([HHMI coral bleaching](https://www.biointeractive.org/classroom-resources/coral-bleaching); [HHMI interactive educator guide](https://www.biointeractive.org/sites/default/files/media/file/2023-03/InteractiveCoralBleaching-Educator-ivideo.pdf); [NASA coral game listing](https://www.nasa.gov/stem-content/coral-bleaching/))

For your next two builds, I would choose **the water-balance extension and paired plant containers**. Both have direct lab connections and small, teachable scopes. Make **genetic rescue the third** when you want to broaden beyond cell physiology. Across all three, use the same short routine: predict, change one variable, run, compare evidence, explain. Labels and shapes should accompany color, pause should freeze the mechanism for discussion, and students should be able to retrieve two runs without copying a large data table. These are design recommendations requiring classroom piloting, not measured claims of learning gains.

The distinctive contribution is a collection of short experiments that expose a mechanism students cannot otherwise watch. Keep each experiment explainable enough that students can criticize its assumptions. That makes the simulation itself part of scientific reasoning, while the lab and authentic datasets remain the evidence against which it is judged.
